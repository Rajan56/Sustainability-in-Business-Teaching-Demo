Does sustainability pay?

Sometimes. Sometimes not. This course is about what it depends on, and who gets to decide.

This page shows how I would teach sustainable business to master’s students: the course design, the way sessions are run, three exercises you can try below, how artificial intelligence fits into learning and assessment, and how I would supervise theses.

The course at a glance

Measuring and governing sustainability in business is a master’s course for students who will one day set targets, choose indicators, answer customers’ data requests or decide whether an initiative is worth its cost. Each session opens with a question on which the research itself disagrees.

Level
Master’s, open to business, engineering and design students
Scope
5 ECTS, six sessions and a group project
Rhythm
Prepare before, work through a case during, reflect after each session
Assessment
Group case with oral defence, individual analytical essay, learning journal
  1. What counts as sustainable business?Whose definition, and what does each one leave out?
  2. Does sustainability pay?When do profit and environmental gain move together, and when do they pull apart?
  3. Measuring what mattersWhat does an organisation see, and what stays invisible, through its indicators?
  4. Reporting, regulation and the value chainWho may ask whom for which information, and with what effect?
  5. Symbolic or substantive?How can we tell a change in practice from a change in paperwork?
  6. Organising for changeGroup projects presented and defended: from measurement to decisions.

How the course is taught

Students do not come to a course to collect conclusions. They come to learn to think about questions that matter to them. The teaching follows from that.

Questions before answers

Every session starts from an open question. Students form their own first view before they meet the literature, so that they notice when the evidence changes their mind.

Evidence that disagrees

Readings are chosen in pairs or triples that reach different conclusions. Students learn to ask why: different samples, measures, settings or assumptions. No study, including the teacher’s own, is treated as the final word.

Observation before conclusion

Students look at the data, the documents and their own first reactions before explaining them. Seeing clearly comes before judging.

Freedom from fear and comparison

A mistake is part of learning, not a verdict on ability. The classroom is a place to think aloud, change one’s mind and disagree with the teacher without cost.

Dialogue, not transfer

I ask before I tell. Discussion is shared inquiry in which students and teacher look at the same problem together.

Students’ own questions

Group projects start from a question the students choose about a real company, so the course connects to what they care about and will meet at work.

Openness does not mean the absence of standards. Every task has published criteria, arguments are judged on their evidence and reasoning, and feedback tells students what to improve and why.

Exercise 1: is there a business case?

Nordvik Metal, a fictional Finnish manufacturer with 140 employees, is considering an investment of €450,000 in heat recovery and electrified drying. It would save about 1,200 MWh of energy and 240 tonnes of CO₂ a year. Move the sliders to see when the investment pays, and what it depends on.

For discussion

  • Which slider changed the answer most, and is that lever in the company’s hands or someone else’s?
  • If the investment does not pay in money terms but cuts emissions, who should carry the cost: owners, customers or the state?
  • What is missing from this model? Think of employees, neighbours and the supplier’s own suppliers.

Exercise 2: where the data stops

You buy components from a supplier with 120 employees. Since 2026, EU rules let suppliers of this size refuse sustainability information requests that go beyond a voluntary standard for SMEs. Choose what you would ask for, then see what the supplier may refuse and what that does to your own picture.

    Simplified for teaching from the basic module of the voluntary standard for SMEs. Not legal advice.

    Supplier’s workload

    About 40 hours a year is what a firm this size can spare for all customer requests.

    Your view of real performance

    Share of what you asked for that arrives as measured data rather than estimates.

    For discussion

    • Does the ceiling protect the supplier, or does it blind the buyer? Could it do both?
    • When measured data is replaced by sector averages, can a real reduction at the supplier still be seen?
    • Where else might the same pressure go: tender criteria, contracts, informal questionnaires?

    Exercise 3: symbolic or substantive?

    Six short scenarios. For each, decide whether you see a real change in practice, a change on paper, or whether you cannot tell yet. There is often more than one defensible answer; the feedback gives the case for each.

    Teaching and learning in the age of AI

    Generative AI is part of the working life students are preparing for, so the course teaches them to use it with judgement rather than pretending it does not exist. The rules are written down, discussed in the first session and applied the same way to everyone, including the teacher.

    Encouraged

    • Generating questions and counter-arguments
    • Explaining a concept in another way
    • Critiquing a draft you wrote yourself
    • Checking analysis code

    Allowed with disclosure

    • Language editing
    • Summarising readings you have already read
    • Help with data analysis
    • A short statement says what was used and how

    Not allowed

    • Submitting generated text as your own analysis
    • Citing sources you have not checked
    • Uploading confidential company data

    Assessment that rewards thinking

    • Group cases end in an oral defence, where students explain choices they made.
    • The essay includes a critique of an AI-generated answer to the same question: what it gets right, what it misses and why.
    • Short process logs show how the work developed, not only the final text.
    • Some in-class tasks use fresh data, so reasoning happens in the room.

    Assessment and criteria

    Group case with oral defence 50 %, individual analytical essay 30 %, learning journal 20 %. The journal asks one question each week: what did I believe before this session, and what do I believe now?

    Supervising master’s theses and supporting doctoral research

    This is how I would supervise. It draws on guiding bachelor students in research methods and academic writing, mentoring multidisciplinary student teams on company challenges, and completing a doctoral dissertation by publication.

    A supervision agreement at the start

    Roles, meeting frequency, response times and what “ready for review” means are agreed in the first meeting.

    Finding the question

    We start from the student’s own curiosity, not a topic handed down, and narrow it together into a researchable question.

    Rhythm and structure

    An agreed meeting schedule and milestones from research plan to final draft, with written feedback that says what to improve and why.

    Independence

    Guiding without taking over. The thesis remains the student’s work, and the student learns to judge their own reasoning.

    Methods and ethics

    Help with choosing a design, survey, case study or document analysis, with data management and with research ethics.

    Writing support

    Early feedback on structure and argument before full drafts, and help turning strong work into a publishable paper.

    Company theses

    Balancing what the company needs with academic quality, and keeping the student’s analysis independent.

    AI in the thesis

    A clear agreement on acceptable use, and disclosure in the thesis itself.

    Peer learning

    Small thesis groups where students present progress and comment on one another’s work.

    Wellbeing and realistic timelines

    Watching for overload, adjusting plans honestly, and making it easy to say “I am stuck” without fear.

    Doctoral research

    Co-supervision, support with mixed methods, and experience of building a dissertation article by article.
